## What is an atmospheric river?

Atmospheric rivers (ARs) are long, narrow filaments of intense water vapor transport in the mid-latitudes. They are thousands of kilometers long, a few hundred kilometers wide, and drift eastward with the extratropical cyclones that accompany them [[3]](#ref-3). On average, an AR carries more water than the Amazon River, and ARs together account for most poleward vapor transport in the mid-latitudes [[4]](#ref-4)[[5]](#ref-5). At landfall they supply much of the precipitation along the coasts they hit, and cause many of the worst floods and wind events there [[6]](#ref-6).

{/* FIGURE SLOT: snapshot (or short animation) of an AR making landfall, e.g. the January 2023 event from Ong & Yang (2024) Fig. 5 / Movie S3. */}

An AR needs two ingredients: **abundant water vapor** and **strong winds**. The standard AR diagnostic that captures both is the **integrated vapor transport** (IVT):

$$
\mathrm{IVT} \equiv \sqrt{\left(-\frac{1}{g}\int_{p_s}^{p_t} qu\,dp\right)^{2} + \left(-\frac{1}{g}\int_{p_s}^{p_t} qv\,dp\right)^{2}}
$$

where $q$ is specific humidity, $(u, v)$ is the horizontal wind, $p_s$ is surface pressure, and $p_t$ (usually 200 hPa) is a level above which vapor is negligible. IVT is the magnitude of the column-integrated vapor flux $q\mathbf{u}$ and has units $\mathrm{kg\,m^{-1}\,s^{-1}}$.

Most AR detection algorithms rely on thresholds of IVT [[7]](#ref-7)[[8]](#ref-8). However, IVT does not have a clean budget equation. Process studies have instead constructed budgets of column water vapor (also known as total precipitable water, up to a factor of the density of liquid water $\rho_w$). This can track where an AR's moisture comes from (mainly convergence) and where it goes (mainly precipitation), but it leaves out the wind entirely. We would like a quantity that combines moisture and wind as IVT does, so that it can detect the same ARs, but has a simple budget equation derived from the equations of motion.

## From momentum to energy

One can think of the vapor transport $q\mathbf{u}$ as a moisture-weighted momentum. In classical mechanics, when momentum is unwieldy, we can turn to energy. Denoting the kinetic energy per unit mass $K \equiv (u^2+v^2)/2$, two "moisture-weighted kinetic energies" are natural:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{kinetic energy of vapor:}\qquad \mathrm{KEV} &\equiv qK \\[4pt]
\text{vapor transport energy:}\qquad \mathrm{VTE} &\equiv q^2K = \tfrac12\,|q\mathbf{u}|^2
\end{aligned}
$$

KEV is the kinetic energy carried by the water vapor. VTE is half the squared norm of the vapor transport $q\mathbf{u}$, playing a similar role to $K$ for $\mathbf{u}$. Both have units of $\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}$. Because $q$, $u$ and $v$ enter VTE with equal powers, as in IVT, VTE is weighted more toward moisture and KEV more toward wind.

<div class="aside">
  A note on names: [[1]](#ref-1) introduced $q^2K$ as <em>vapor kinetic energy</em> (VKE). [[2]](#ref-2) renamed it vapor transport energy (VTE) to distinguish it from KEV, and "VKE" now refers to the framework.
</div>

The column integrals, in $\mathrm{J\,m^{-2}}$, are

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{IKEV} &\equiv -\frac{1}{g}\int_{p_s}^{p_t} qK\,dp, &
\mathrm{IVTE} &\equiv -\frac{1}{g}\int_{p_s}^{p_t} q^2K\,dp.
\end{aligned}
$$

Unlike IVT, IKEV and IVTE take the (squared) norm of the winds before taking the vertical integral. This allows us to derive a clean budget equation from the budget equations of momentum and water vapor. 

## Detected ARs are similar to those from IVT

Below, we show the similarities and differences between ARs detected from IKEV and IVTE and those detected from IVT, using a Laplacian thresholding algorithm. The Laplacian thresholds for IKEV and IVTE are chosen so that the global climatological frequency of ARs approximately matches the detection from IVT. With IVTE, global AR frequency changes by only 2–4 days per year, less than the ~10 days per year separating two standard IVT algorithms [[1]](#ref-1). IKEV detects slightly fewer ARs at low latitudes and more at high southern latitudes, where the jet is strong [[2]](#ref-2).

<Derivation title="Details on the AR detection algorithm">

We detect ARs with the Laplacian method of [TempestExtremes](https://github.com/ClimateGlobalChange/tempestextremes) `DetectBlobs`, following [[1]](#ref-1). For a column field $\phi$ (IVT, IKEV or IVTE), a grid point is an AR point if

$$
\nabla^2\phi < C_\phi
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
|\vartheta| \ge 20^\circ,
$$

where $\vartheta$ is latitude and $C_\phi$ is a threshold. An AR is a blob of at least 50 such grid points,
connected through shared edges. The Laplacian thresholds are

$$
\begin{aligned}
C_{\mathrm{IVT}} &= -4\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-1}\,s^{-1}\,rad^{-2}}, \\[4pt]
C_{\mathrm{IKEV}} &= -4.6\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-2}\,rad^{-2}}, \\[4pt]
C_{\mathrm{IVTE}} &= -2\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-2}\,rad^{-2}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The IVT threshold is the usual TempestExtremes value. The IKEV and IVTE thresholds are chosen to approximately match the global AR frequency of the IVT detection.

</Derivation>

## The budget equations

The KEV and VTE budgets follow from the moisture and momentum equations by the product rule (derivation below). In flux form [[2]](#ref-2):

$$
\tc{tend}{\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{KEV}}{\partial t}}
=
\term{hflux}{\underbrace{-\nabla_p\!\cdot\!\left(\mathbf{u}\,\mathrm{KEV}\right)}_{\text{horizontal flux}}}
\;\term{vflux}{\underbrace{-\,\frac{\partial\left(\omega\,\mathrm{KEV}\right)}{\partial p}}_{\text{vertical flux}}}
\;\term{pe}{\underbrace{-\,q\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla_p\Phi}_{\text{PE}\,\to\,\text{KE}}}
\;\term{cond}{\underbrace{+\;K\,S}_{\text{vapor sources/sinks}}}
\;\term{fric}{\underbrace{+\;q\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{F}}_{\substack{\text{sub-grid momentum}\\\text{sources/sinks}}}}
$$

$$
\tc{tend}{\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{VTE}}{\partial t}}
=
\term{hflux}{-\nabla_p\!\cdot\!\left(\mathbf{u}\,\mathrm{VTE}\right)}
\;\term{vflux}{-\,\frac{\partial\left(\omega\,\mathrm{VTE}\right)}{\partial p}}
\;\term{pe}{-\,q^2\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla_p\Phi}
\;\term{cond}{+\;2qK\,S}
\;\term{fric}{+\;q^2\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{F}}
$$

Here $\omega$ is pressure velocity, $\Phi$ is geopotential, $S$ is the net vapor source, and $\mathbf{F}$ is the parameterized sub-grid momentum forcing, which in MERRA-2 comprises turbulence, gravity-wave drag and convection. The two equations differ only in their moisture weighting.

<TermPanels>
  <Term kind="hflux" name="Horizontal flux convergence">

  Horizontal winds carry KEV and VTE through the AR. Convergence downstream and divergence upstream move the AR. In some regions, net convergence also adds to its growth.

  </Term>
  <Term kind="vflux" name="Vertical flux convergence">

  Redistributes KEV and VTE vertically, but nearly vanishes in the column integral, since $\omega \approx 0$ at the surface and $q \approx 0$ at $p_t$.

  </Term>
  <Term kind="pe" name="Conversion of potential to kinetic energy">

  Flow down the geopotential gradient accelerates. Geostrophic flow runs along $\Phi$ contours and does no work, so only the ageostrophic (cross-contour) flow converts PE to KE. This is the process that drives low-level jets, and it is absent from the water vapor budget.

  </Term>
  <Term kind="cond" name="Vapor sources & sinks">

  This term includes all sources and sinks of water vapor, including condensation, evaporation and chemistry. The main component is condensation, which acts as a sink of KEV and VTE.

  </Term>
  <Term kind="fric" name="Sub-grid momentum sources & sinks">

  The term includes all sub-grid momentum forcing, which in MERRA-2 comprises turbulence, gravity-wave drag and convection. The main component is turbulent drag, concentrated in the boundary layer, which slows the wind and removes KEV and VTE.

  </Term>
</TermPanels>

### Integrating over the column

With $\langle\,\cdot\,\rangle \equiv -\frac{1}{g}\int_{p_s}^{p_t}(\,\cdot\,)\,dp$, the column budgets are, to leading order [[1]](#ref-1):

$$
\tc{tend}{\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{IKEV}}{\partial t}}
\approx
\tc{hflux}{-\big\langle\nabla_p\!\cdot\!(\mathbf{u}\,\mathrm{KEV})\big\rangle}
\;\tc{pe}{-\,\big\langle q\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla_p\Phi\big\rangle}
\;\tc{cond}{+\,\big\langle K S\big\rangle}
\;\tc{fric}{+\,\big\langle q\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{F}\big\rangle}
$$

$$
\tc{tend}{\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{IVTE}}{\partial t}}
\approx
\tc{hflux}{-\big\langle\nabla_p\!\cdot\!(\mathbf{u}\,\mathrm{VTE})\big\rangle}
\;\tc{pe}{-\,\big\langle q^2\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla_p\Phi\big\rangle}
\;\tc{cond}{+\,\big\langle 2q K S\big\rangle}
\;\tc{fric}{+\,\big\langle q^2 \,\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{F}\big\rangle}
$$


<Derivation title="Derivation of the budget equations">

Let $K \equiv |\mathbf{u}|^2/2$ be the kinetic energy per unit mass, and define
$\mathrm{KEV} \equiv qK$ and $\mathrm{VTE} \equiv q^2K = |q\mathbf{u}|^2/2$.
With the material derivative $\frac{D}{Dt} \equiv \frac{\partial}{\partial t} + \mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla_p + \omega\frac{\partial}{\partial p}$,
the moisture and horizontal momentum equations of the hydrostatic primitive equations are

$$
\frac{Dq}{Dt} = S, \qquad
\frac{D\mathbf{u}}{Dt} = -f \mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{u} - \nabla_p\Phi + \mathbf{F},
$$

where $S$ denotes sources and sinks of water vapor, $f = 2\Omega\sin\vartheta$ is the Coriolis parameter ($\Omega$ is Earth\'s rotation rate and $\vartheta$ is latitude), $\Phi$ is the geopotential, and $\mathbf{F}$ is the sum of sub-grid momentum sources and sinks.

**Kinetic energy.** Dotting the momentum equation with $\mathbf{u}$ removes the Coriolis term, since $\mathbf{u}\cdot(\mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{u}) = 0$:

$$
\frac{DK}{Dt} = -\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla_p\Phi + \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{F}.
$$

**Product rule.** Combining with the moisture equation,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{D}{Dt} \mathrm{KEV} &= K\frac{Dq}{Dt} + q\frac{DK}{Dt} = K S - q\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla_p\Phi + q\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{F}, \\[4pt]
\frac{D}{Dt} \mathrm{VTE} &= 2qK\frac{Dq}{Dt} + q^2\frac{DK}{Dt} = 2qK S - q^2\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla_p\Phi + q^2\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{F}.
\end{aligned}
$$

**Flux form.** By continuity, $\nabla_p\cdot\mathbf{u} + \partial\omega/\partial p = 0$, so for any $X$,

$$
\frac{\partial X}{\partial t} = -\nabla_p\cdot(\mathbf{u}X) - \frac{\partial(\omega X)}{\partial p} + \frac{DX}{Dt}.
$$

This gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{KEV}}{\partial t} &= -\nabla_p\cdot\left(\mathbf{u}\,\mathrm{KEV}\right) - \frac{\partial\left(\omega\,\mathrm{KEV}\right)}{\partial p} - q\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla_p\Phi + K S + q\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{F}, \\[4pt]
\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{VTE}}{\partial t} &= -\nabla_p\cdot\left(\mathbf{u}\,\mathrm{VTE}\right) - \frac{\partial\left(\omega\,\mathrm{VTE}\right)}{\partial p} - q^2\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla_p\Phi + 2qK S + q^2\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{F}.
\end{aligned}
$$


**Column integral.** Apply $\langle\,\cdot\,\rangle \equiv -\frac{1}{g}\int_{p_s}^{p_t}(\,\cdot\,)\,dp$ to each term of the flux-form budgets,
so that $\mathrm{IKEV} = \langle\mathrm{KEV}\rangle$ and $\mathrm{IVTE} = \langle\mathrm{VTE}\rangle$. Because the surface
pressure $p_s$ varies in time, the tendency term picks up a surface contribution,

$$
\left\langle\frac{\partial X}{\partial t}\right\rangle = \frac{\partial\langle X\rangle}{\partial t} - \frac{X_s}{g}\frac{\partial p_s}{\partial t},
$$

which is small because surface pressure tendencies are small. The vertical flux term integrates to its values at
the column boundaries, which nearly vanish because $\omega \approx 0$ at the surface and $q \approx 0$ at $p_t$.
Dropping both, the column budgets are, to leading order,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{IKEV}}{\partial t} &\approx -\big\langle\nabla_p\cdot(\mathbf{u}\,\mathrm{KEV})\big\rangle - \big\langle q\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla_p\Phi\big\rangle + \big\langle K S\big\rangle + \big\langle q\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{F}\big\rangle, \\[4pt]
\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{IVTE}}{\partial t} &\approx -\big\langle\nabla_p\cdot(\mathbf{u}\,\mathrm{VTE})\big\rangle - \big\langle q^2\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla_p\Phi\big\rangle + \big\langle 2qK S\big\rangle + \big\langle q^2\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{F}\big\rangle.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div className="aside">

In MERRA-2, $S$ combines moist physics, turbulence (including surface evaporation) and chemistry,
and $\mathbf{F}$ combines turbulence, gravity-wave drag and convection. There is also a small contribution from data-assimilation increments.
</div>

</Derivation>

## Code

We are developing an open-source Python package for these diagnostics, [vke-budgets](https://github.com/ychnli/vke-budgets). It takes the winds, humidity, geopotential and surface pressure from a reanalysis product or model output and computes every term of the column-integrated KEV and VTE budgets on its grid. It currently reads MERRA-2, whose physics tendencies give the vapor and sub-grid momentum sources/sinks directly. Support for ERA5 is forthcoming.

For example, the KEV budget of one day of MERRA-2 over the northeast Pacific:

```python
from vke_budgets import budget
from vke_budgets.sources import merra2

# The MERRA-2 files of 4 January 2023, kept in one directory per year
day = merra2.open_day("20230104", "/path/to/merra2")

# Every term of the KEV and VTE budgets, for the eight 3-hour windows of the day
result = budget.compute(day, n_workers=3)

# The KEV budget by group, over the northeast Pacific
kev_budget = budget.groups(result, "KEV").sel(lat=slice(15, 60), lon=slice(-170, -110))
kev_budget
```
